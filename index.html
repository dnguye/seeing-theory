A visual introduction to probability

Randomness is all around us.

Probability theory is the mathematical framework for reasoning about chance events in a logically sound way. This chapter builds the basic vocabulary of that framework, and every idea in it comes with an experiment you can run yourself.

Start with a coin

Chance events

The probability of an event is a number that says how likely that event is to occur. It always lies between 0 and 1: a probability of 0 means the event is impossible, and a probability of 1 means it is certain.

The classic example is a coin toss. A fair coin has two outcomes, heads and tails, and each has probability ½. Any single flip is unpredictable. But flip the coin many times and something remarkable happens: the fraction of heads settles down. As the number of flips increases, the long-run frequency of heads is bound to get closer and closer to 50%. That stabilisation is the bridge between chance and mathematics. Probability is what long-run frequency converges to.

Not every coin is fair. Drag the weight slider to make heads more or less likely and watch the running frequency chase a different target. Assigning a number to each outcome, 1 for heads and 0 for tails, turns the flip into a random variable, an idea we return to below.

Check yourself: a coin lands heads 7 times in 10 flips. Is it biased?

You can't tell from ten flips. A fair coin gives 7 or more heads in 10 flips about 17% of the time. Small samples are noisy; the law of large numbers only bites when the sample is large.

Sample spaces and events

Before we can compute probabilities we have to be precise about what can happen. The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment is its sample space, written Ω. For one roll of a die, Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. An event is any subset of the sample space: “the roll is even” is the event {2, 4, 6}.

The probability of an event is the sum of the probabilities of the outcomes it contains. When every outcome is equally likely, that is just the number of outcomes in the event divided by the size of the sample space.

P(E)= ∑ω∈E P(ω)

Two consequences follow at once. The whole sample space has probability 1, because something must happen. And the probability that an event does not happen, its complement, is 1 − P(E). Tap the faces below to build an event, then roll to see how often it occurs.

Expectation

The expectation of a random variable is a number that attempts to capture the center of that random variable's distribution. It can be interpreted as the long-run average of many independent samples from the given distribution. More precisely, for a discrete random variable X the expectation is the probability-weighted sum of its possible values:

E[X]= ∑x∈𝒳 xP(x)

For a fair die every face has probability 1/6, so E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value the die can never actually show. Expectation is a center of mass, not a most likely outcome.

Roll the die and watch the sample mean converge to 3.5. Then drag the bars to weight the die and see the expectation move with it. The running average will follow.

Check yourself: a lottery ticket costs $2 and pays $1,000,000 with probability one in a million. What is the expected value of buying one?

E[payout] = 1,000,000 × 0.000001 = $1, so the expected value of the purchase is 1 − 2 = −$1. On average, every ticket loses a dollar, even though every individual ticket either loses $2 or wins big.

Variance

Whereas expectation provides a measure of centrality, the variance of a random variable quantifies the spread of that random variable's distribution. It is the expectation of the squared deviation from the mean:

Var(X)= E[(X−E[X])2]

Squaring does two jobs. It makes every deviation positive, so spread above and below the mean cannot cancel out, and it weights large deviations more heavily than small ones. Because variance is measured in squared units, we usually also report its square root, the standard deviation, which lives on the same scale as the data.

Draw cards from the deck below. Each draw contributes one squared deviation (x − μ)², plotted as a grey dot. The running average of those (blue) converges to the true variance (green). Remove cards to change the deck and watch both the mean and the spread respond.

The law of large numbers

You have now seen the same phenomenon three times: sample frequencies approach probabilities, sample means approach expectations, and sample variances approach the true variance. The law of large numbers is the theorem that guarantees it. Take independent samples from any distribution with a finite mean μ; then the average of the first n samples converges to μ as n grows.

It says nothing about any single sample, and nothing about short runs. Below, twenty gamblers each flip the same coin. For the first few flips their fortunes scatter wildly; after a thousand they are nearly indistinguishable. The convergence is also slow: the typical error shrinks like 1/√n, so ten times more flips buys only about three times more precision.

Streaks and the gambler's fallacy

Randomness does not look the way people expect. Ask someone to write down 200 “random” coin flips and they will avoid long runs; a real coin does not. In 200 fair flips the longest run of heads is usually six to eight long, and a run of ten is not rare. A useful rule of thumb: the longest run in n fair flips is about log₂ n.

This feeds a persistent illusion. After five heads in a row, tails feels “due”. But the coin has no memory. Each flip is independent: the probability of heads is the same no matter what came before. That is the gambler's fallacy, and you can test it directly below by counting what happened after every run of k heads.

Probability as area

So far our sample spaces have been short lists. Probability also applies to continuous outcomes, and there it behaves like area. Throw a dart at random at a square board, so that every point is equally likely; then the probability that it lands in some region is the region's area divided by the area of the square.

This turns probability into a measuring tool. A circle inscribed in a square covers π/4 of it, so the fraction of darts that land inside the circle estimates π/4, and four times that fraction estimates π. Estimating a quantity by simulating random samples is called the Monte Carlo method. It is one of the most widely used ideas in modern computation, from physics to finance to graphics, and the law of large numbers is what makes it work.

Key ideas from this chapter

The vocabulary you'll need for everything that follows.

Outcome
One possible result of an experiment, such as rolling a 4.
Sample space, Ω
The set of all possible outcomes.
Event
Any set of outcomes. “Even” is the event {2, 4, 6}.
Probability
A number in [0, 1] for each event, equal to the sum of the probabilities of its outcomes. It is what long-run frequency converges to.
Complement
The event “not E”, with probability 1 − P(E).
Random variable
A number attached to each outcome, such as 1 for heads and 0 for tails.
Expectation, E[X]
The probability-weighted average of a random variable's values. Its long-run sample mean.
Variance and standard deviation
Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] measures spread; the standard deviation is its square root, in the original units.
Independence
Two events are independent when one happening tells you nothing about the other. Successive coin flips are independent.
Law of large numbers
Averages of independent samples converge to the expectation. Error shrinks roughly like 1/√n.
Monte Carlo method
Estimating a quantity by simulating random samples and averaging, justified by the law of large numbers.
