Probability & Statistics 1 · Chapter 4

Continuous distributions

Heights, waiting times and measurement errors don't come in steps. For continuous random variables probability lives in areas under a curve, and a few curves, above all the normal, describe an astonishing share of the world.

In this chapter

    Density and area

    A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval, so no single value can carry positive probability: there are too many of them. Instead, probability is spread out according to a probability density function f, and the probability of landing in an interval is the area under f over that interval:

    P(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

    The total area is 1. The density itself is not a probability, and can exceed 1; only areas are probabilities. The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) accumulates area from the left, so a probability over an interval is just a difference of two values of F. Expectation and variance become integrals: E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx.

    Try this

    On the normal, set a = b. The probability is 0 even though the density there is 0.4. Then use the empirical-rule chips: about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of a normal distribution lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean.

    Uniform and exponential

    The uniform distribution on [a, b] is the continuous version of “equally likely”: flat density 1/(b − a), mean (a + b)/2, variance (b − a)²/12. It is what a random number generator produces, and every other distribution can be built from it.

    The exponential distribution describes waiting times between events that occur at a steady rate λ, the continuous partner of the Poisson: if events per hour are Poisson(λ), the wait for the next one is Exponential(λ) with mean 1/λ. Like the geometric distribution it is memoryless: having already waited ten minutes does not shorten the expected remaining wait.

    The normal distribution

    The bell curve. A normal random variable with mean μ and standard deviation σ has density

    f(x)=1σ2πe−(x−μ)2/2σ2

    Changing μ slides the curve; changing σ stretches it. Every normal distribution is the same shape, so any normal question can be converted to the standard normal (μ = 0, σ = 1) by standardising: z = (x − μ)/σ counts how many standard deviations x lies from the mean. That is why one table of the standard normal CDF once served all of statistics.

    The normal distribution matters not because nature prefers it but because of the central limit theorem: sums and averages of many small independent effects are approximately normal whatever the effects' own distributions. Chapter 6 shows it happening.

    Check yourself: heights are Normal(170, 8²). What fraction of people are taller than 186 cm?

    z = (186 − 170)/8 = 2. About 2.3% of a normal distribution lies more than two standard deviations above the mean, so roughly 2.3%. Set μ = 0, σ = 1 in the density lab above with a = 2, b = 4 to read it off.

    Gamma and beta

    Two flexible families finish the chapter. The gamma distribution, with shape k and scale θ, lives on positive numbers and includes the exponential (k = 1) and chi-square (θ = 2, k = df/2) as special cases. The sum of k independent Exponential(1/θ) waits is Gamma(k, θ), so it models the time until the k-th event. Mean kθ, variance kθ².

    The beta distribution lives on [0, 1], which makes it the natural distribution for a probability. Beta(1, 1) is uniform; Beta(α, β) with large parameters concentrates near α/(α + β). It reappears in chapter 14 as the Bayesian's way of expressing belief about a coin's bias.
