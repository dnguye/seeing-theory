Probability & Statistics 1 · Chapter 6

Limit theorems

Two theorems explain why statistics works at all. The law of large numbers says averages settle down. The central limit theorem says how they wobble on the way: like a bell curve, no matter where the data came from.

In this chapter

    The law of large numbers, and when it fails

    Chapter 1 showed the law for a coin: the running frequency of heads converges to P(heads). The general statement is about any random variable with a finite mean μ. If X₁, X₂, … are independent copies of it, the sample mean (X₁ + ··· + Xₙ)/n converges to μ as n → ∞.

    The condition matters. The Cauchy distribution, a bell-shaped curve with much heavier tails than the normal, has no mean at all: its tails are so heavy that the integral ∫ x f(x) dx does not converge. Averages of Cauchy samples never settle. Every so often a single enormous value drags the running mean across the chart, and averaging a million of them is no better than looking at one.

    Try this

    Run the die a few times and watch all six paths funnel into 3.5. Then choose Cauchy and run it several times. Notice that the running mean of a Cauchy sample has exactly the same distribution as a single Cauchy draw.

    The central limit theorem

    Take n independent draws from any distribution with mean μ and finite variance σ², and compute their mean. That sample mean is itself random. The central limit theorem says its distribution is approximately normal, with the same mean μ and a much smaller standard deviation:

    X¯≈N(μ,σ2n)

    The parent distribution can be flat, skewed, two-humped or a die; the averages still pile up in a bell. How large n must be depends on the parent: for a symmetric parent a handful suffices, for a strongly skewed one you may need 30 or more. The lab draws samples, computes their means, and stacks them into a histogram beside the predicted normal curve.

    Try this

    Choose the exponential parent with n = 2 and draw 1,000 samples: still skewed. Raise n to 30: the bell is there. Then choose Cauchy: no n is enough, because the theorem needs a finite variance.

    Check yourself: why does the SD of the sample mean shrink like 1/√n rather than 1/n?

    Variances of independent terms add. The sum of n terms has variance nσ², so the mean has variance nσ²/n² = σ²/n, and standard deviation σ/√n. Quadrupling the sample halves the spread.

    The normal approximation to the binomial

    A binomial count is a sum of n Bernoulli variables, so the central limit theorem applies directly: for large n, Bin(n, p) is approximately Normal(np, np(1 − p)). Historically this was the first central limit theorem, proved by de Moivre in 1733 for a fair coin, and it turned tedious binomial sums into a single table lookup.

    The approximation is good when both np and n(1 − p) are at least about 10. For small p the binomial is skewed and the Poisson is the better approximation. The dashed curve below is the matching normal; slide n and p and judge the fit yourself.
