Categorical data

Not all data are measurements. Votes, die faces, yes-or-no answers are counts in categories, and the chi-square statistic is the standard way to ask whether observed counts match what a hypothesis predicts.

In this chapter

    Goodness of fit

    Roll a die 120 times. A fair die predicts 20 of each face, but you will not get exactly 20. How far from 20 is too far? Pearson's chi-square statistic sums the squared gaps between observed and expected counts, each scaled by the expected count:

    χ2=∑i(Oi−Ei)2Ei

    If the die is fair and the expected counts are not too small, this statistic follows approximately a chi-square distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom, where k is the number of categories (the −1 because the counts must sum to n). Large values are evidence against fairness. The lab rolls a die that you can quietly load, and shows both the single test and how the statistic behaves over a thousand repetitions.

    Try this

    Leave the die fair and repeat 1,000 times: about 5% of experiments reject anyway, the Type I rate. Add 0.10 extra weight on the 6 with n = 120: the test catches it only sometimes. Raise n to 600 and it catches it nearly every time. Bias that is invisible in 120 rolls is obvious in 600.

    Tests of independence

    The same statistic tests whether two categorical variables are related. Cross-tabulate the sample into a contingency table, say two groups by a yes-or-no answer. If group and answer were independent, the expected count in each cell would be (row total × column total)/n, the product rule from chapter 2 applied to the marginal proportions. Compare with the observed counts using χ², now with (rows − 1)(columns − 1) degrees of freedom: one, for a 2 × 2 table.

    A small p-value says the answer rate differs between groups by more than sampling noise explains. It says nothing about why, and nothing about the size of the difference: with n large enough, a trivial difference is significant, so always report the proportions alongside the p-value.

    Try this

    Make the two conditional probabilities equal, so the variables are truly independent, and draw samples repeatedly: p bounces around uniformly and dips below 0.05 about one time in twenty. Then set them to 0.50 and 0.55 with n = 2,000 and see how a 5-point difference becomes “significant”.
