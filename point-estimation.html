Probability & Statistics 2 · Chapter 8

Point estimation

An estimator is a recipe for guessing a parameter from data. Since the data are random, so is the guess. Good estimators are the ones whose guesses land close to the truth on average, and reliably.

In this chapter

    Bias, variance and mean squared error

    An estimator θ̂ of a parameter θ is a statistic, so it has a sampling distribution. Two features of that distribution matter. Its bias is how far its centre is from the truth, E[θ̂] − θ. Its variance is how much it scatters from sample to sample. Both hurt, and they combine into one overall measure:

    MSE(θ^)=E[(θ^−θ)2]=bias2+variance

    The dartboard makes it visual. An unbiased estimator with high variance sprays darts around the bullseye; a biased one with low variance clusters tightly in the wrong place. Sometimes the second is better: a little bias can buy a large reduction in variance. Unbiasedness is a virtue, not the only one.

    Try this

    Compare bias 0, SD 1.0 (MSE = 1.00) against bias 0.5, SD 0.5 (MSE = 0.50). The biased estimator is twice as accurate on average. Then take 100 estimates of each and look at how the average lands.

    Maximum likelihood

    Where do estimators come from? The most general recipe is maximum likelihood. Write down the probability of the observed data as a function of the unknown parameter, the likelihood function, and choose the parameter value that makes the data most probable. For h heads in n flips the likelihood is

    L(p)=ph(1−p)n−h

    and it peaks at p̂ = h/n, the sample proportion. Because products are awkward we usually maximise the logarithm instead; it has the same peak. Maximum likelihood estimators are consistent, approximately normal for large samples, and as precise as any estimator can be, which is why they are the default in most of statistics.

    A simpler recipe, the method of moments, matches sample averages to population moments: set the sample mean equal to E[X] and solve. For the coin both recipes give h/n; for other models they differ.

    Try this

    Keep pressing “Flip 10 more”. The peak sharpens as n grows, which is the likelihood's way of saying the estimate is becoming more certain. Switch to the log scale to see the curve become a parabola near its peak.

    Why the sample variance divides by n − 1

    The obvious estimator of the population variance is the average squared deviation from the sample mean, dividing by n. It is biased: the sample mean is closer to the sample points than the true mean is, so squared deviations from it come out slightly too small, by a factor of exactly (n − 1)/n. Dividing by n − 1 instead corrects this, and that corrected version is what statistical software calls the sample variance s².

    The lab draws many samples from a Normal(0, 1) population, so the truth is σ² = 1, and compares the two estimators. The biased one centres below 1; the corrected one centres on it. The gap is large for small n and negligible for large n.

    Check yourself: with n = 5, what is the expected value of the ÷ n estimator?

    σ²(n − 1)/n = 1 × 4/5 = 0.8. Draw 1,000 samples and check that the mean of the estimates sits near 0.8, while the ÷ (n − 1) version sits near 1.
