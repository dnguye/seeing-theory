Joint distributions

Most interesting questions involve two or more random variables at once: height and weight, today's price and tomorrow's. A joint distribution describes them together, and covariance measures how they move together.

In this chapter

    Joint, marginal and conditional distributions

    For two discrete random variables the joint PMF gives P(X = x, Y = y) for every pair. Summing across a row or a column recovers the distribution of one variable on its own, the marginal distribution, so called because those sums were traditionally written in the margins of the table. Fixing one variable and renormalising a row gives the conditional distribution of the other:

    P(Y=y|X=x)=P(X=x,Y=y)P(X=x)

    X and Y are independent when the joint PMF factors into the product of the marginals for every pair. In the table below, X is the first of two dice and Y is their sum. They are clearly dependent: a high first die rules out low sums.

    Covariance and correlation

    Variance measured how one variable spreads around its mean. Covariance measures whether two variables deviate from their means in the same direction:

    Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−EX)(Y−EY)]=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]

    Positive covariance: when X is above its mean, Y tends to be too. Its units are awkward (centimetres times kilograms), so we divide by both standard deviations to get the correlation ρ, a pure number between −1 and 1. ρ = ±1 means the points lie exactly on a line; ρ = 0 means no linear relationship, which is weaker than independence, since a perfect U-shaped relationship also has ρ = 0.

    Independent variables have zero covariance, and that is what makes variances add: Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y).

    Sums of random variables

    Expectation is always additive, E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], whether or not X and Y are independent. The distribution of a sum is harder: for independent variables it is the convolution of the two distributions, which for dice means counting the ways to reach each total.

    Adding independent copies of the same distribution smooths it out rapidly. One die is flat, two make a triangle, three make a curve that already looks like a bell. This regularity is the seed of the central limit theorem in the next chapter.

    Check yourself: what are the mean and variance of the sum of 10 dice?
