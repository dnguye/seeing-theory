Confidence intervals

A point estimate is a single number that is almost certainly wrong. An interval estimate admits the uncertainty: a range of values, built so that it captures the truth a known fraction of the time.

In this chapter

    What 95% confidence means

    Since x̄ is approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n), it lands within 1.96 standard errors of μ about 95% of the time. Turn that around: the interval x̄ ± 1.96 σ/√n contains μ about 95% of the time. That interval is a 95% confidence interval, and in general

    x¯±zα/2σn

    The 95% is a property of the procedure, not of any one interval. A particular interval either contains μ or it doesn't; we just don't know which. What we know is that intervals built this way succeed 95% of the time in the long run. The lab draws 100 samples, builds 100 intervals, and colours the ones that miss.

    Try this

    Lower the confidence level to 80%: the intervals shrink and about 20 of every 100 turn red. Raise n from 15 to 60: the intervals halve in width and the coverage stays 95%. Confidence is about how often you are right; width is about how much you have said.

    When σ is unknown: t intervals

    The formula above needs σ, which we almost never know. Substituting the sample standard deviation s adds noise, and for small samples the normal critical value 1.96 is then too small: the intervals miss more often than advertised. The fix is the t distribution of chapter 7. Use the critical value from t with n − 1 degrees of freedom instead:

    x¯±tn−1,α/2sn

    With n = 5 the 95% t critical value is 2.78 rather than 1.96, so the interval is 40% wider. Switch the lab above between the three methods with n = 5 and compare the long-run coverage: z with σ known is exact, t is exact, and z with s falls short.

    Check yourself: with n = 3 and the “z with s” method, roughly what coverage do you get instead of 95%?

    About 86%. Set n = 3, choose “z with s” and run several batches; the tally will settle in the mid-80s. Then choose t and watch it return to 95%.

    The bootstrap

    The interval formulas rely on the sampling distribution of the statistic, which we could derive for the mean but not for, say, a median or a ratio. The bootstrap sidesteps the derivation with computation. Treat the sample as if it were the population, draw new samples of size n from it with replacement, and compute the statistic each time. The spread of those bootstrap statistics estimates the spread of the real sampling distribution.

    A 95% bootstrap interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the bootstrap values. Below, one sample of 30 skewed observations is all we have; a thousand resamples give a standard error and an interval without any formula.

    Try this

    Resample once, several times, and watch which points were picked and how many times. Then resample 1,000 times and compare the bootstrap standard error with the formula s/√n: they agree closely for the mean, which is reassuring, but the bootstrap would have worked just as well for the median.
