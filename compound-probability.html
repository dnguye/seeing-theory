Compound probability

One event is easy. Real questions involve several: this or that, this and that, this given that. This chapter builds the algebra of events and ends with the most useful formula in the subject, Bayes' theorem.

In this chapter

    Events as sets

    Because events are sets of outcomes, the language of sets is the language of compound events. The union A ∪ B is the event that A or B (or both) occurs. The intersection A ∩ B is the event that both occur. The complement Aᶜ is the event that A does not occur.

    Probabilities of unions have a trap. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice, so it has to be subtracted once:

    P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)

    When A and B cannot both happen, they are mutually exclusive, the overlap is empty, and the probabilities simply add. Below, every cell is one equally likely outcome; resize the events and their overlap, pick a region, and check the formula against random picks.

    Try this

    Make the overlap 0. Now A and B are mutually exclusive and P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) exactly. Then make A a subset of B (overlap equal to the size of A): P(A ∪ B) = P(B).

    Counting

    When outcomes are equally likely, probability is a counting problem: the number of favourable outcomes over the total. Two counting patterns come up constantly. A permutation is an ordered arrangement of k items chosen from n; there are n(n − 1)···(n − k + 1) = n!/(n − k)! of them. A combination is an unordered selection; each set of k items has been counted k! times among the permutations, so there are

    (nk)=n!k!(n−k)!

    The question to ask is always: does the order matter? A committee of three does not care who was picked first; a podium of gold, silver and bronze does.

    Check yourself: what is the probability that a random 5-card hand from a 52-card deck contains all four aces?

    Order doesn't matter, so count hands: C(52, 5) = 2,598,960 in total. Hands with all four aces are the four aces plus any one of the other 48 cards: 48 of them. Probability 48 / 2,598,960 ≈ 0.0000185, about 1 in 54,000.

    Conditional probability and independence

    New information shrinks the sample space. If you learn that event B has occurred, the outcomes outside B are ruled out, and the probability of A must be recomputed relative to what remains:

    P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)

    Rearranged, this is the multiplication rule, P(A ∩ B) = P(B) P(A | B): the probability that both happen is the probability of the first times the probability of the second given the first.

    Sometimes learning B changes nothing: P(A | B) = P(A). Then A and B are independent, and the multiplication rule simplifies to P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). Independence is a statement about information, not about whether the events can happen together. Explore both ideas with two dice below.

    Try this

    Set A = “sum is 7” and B = “first die is 6”. They are independent: whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second die makes the sum 7. Now change A to “sum ≥ 10” and see the dependence appear.

    Bayes' theorem

    The multiplication rule can be written in either order: P(A ∩ B) = P(B) P(A | B) = P(A) P(B | A). Dividing through gives a way to reverse a conditional probability:

    P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(B)

    This matters because the two directions feel similar but can be wildly different. A medical test may detect a disease 90% of the time when it is present, P(positive | sick) = 0.9, and yet a positive result may mean only a small chance of illness, because the disease is rare and false alarms among the many healthy people outnumber the true positives.

    The clearest way to see it is to imagine 1,000 people and count. That is what the lab does: the sliders set the prevalence and the test's accuracy, and the dots show who ends up in each group.

    Try this

    With prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90% and specificity 90%, only about 8% of positives are sick. Raise the prevalence to 30% and watch that number jump. Then push specificity to 99%: cutting false alarms helps far more than raising sensitivity does.

    Check yourself: which changes P(sick | positive) more, sensitivity 90 → 99% or specificity 90 → 99%?

    At 1% prevalence, specificity. Raising sensitivity adds at most one more true positive per 1,000 people; raising specificity removes about 89 false positives. The base rate decides which error dominates.
