Probability & Statistics 2 · Chapter 7

Sampling distributions

Probability runs forward: given the distribution, what will the data look like? Statistics runs backward: given the data, what was the distribution? The bridge between them is the idea that a statistic computed from a random sample is itself a random variable.

In this chapter

    From population to sample

    A population is the whole collection we care about, described by parameters such as its mean μ and standard deviation σ. We rarely see it. Instead we draw a sample of n observations and compute statistics from it: the sample mean x̄, the sample standard deviation s, the median, the maximum.

    Draw another sample and every statistic changes. The distribution of a statistic across all possible samples is its sampling distribution, and it is the key object of the second course: it tells us how far a statistic is likely to be from the parameter it estimates. Below, the population is adult heights, Normal(170, 8²). Draw samples and watch each statistic scatter.

    Try this

    Draw 1,000 samples of the mean, then of the median. Both centre on 170, but the median's histogram is wider: for normal data the mean is the more precise estimator. Then look at the maximum, which is biased far above 170 and drifts higher as n grows.

    The distribution of the sample mean

    For the sample mean, the central limit theorem gives the whole story: x̄ is approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n). Its standard deviation σ/√n is called the standard error. The formula has two lessons. Precision improves with sample size, but only as the square root: to halve the standard error you must quadruple n. And the spread of the population sets the scale of everything.

    The same machinery applies to any parent distribution, which is why the lab lets you pick a skewed one and watch the bell appear anyway.

    The t distribution

    Standardising the sample mean gives z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n), which is standard normal. In practice σ is unknown and we substitute the sample standard deviation s. The result, t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n), is no longer normal: s is itself noisy, and when it comes out small the ratio is inflated. Gosset, writing as “Student” in 1908, worked out the distribution while doing quality control at the Guinness brewery.

    The t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom looks like the standard normal but with heavier tails. Extreme values are more likely because a small sample can underestimate σ. As the degrees of freedom grow, s becomes reliable and t merges into the normal; by df = 30 the two are close.

    Try this

    Set df = 1: the tails are so heavy that the variance is infinite (this is the Cauchy distribution from chapter 6). Move to df = 5, then 30, and watch the curve approach the dashed normal.

    Chi-square and F

    Two more sampling distributions complete the toolkit. A sum of k squared standard normal variables has a chi-square distribution with k degrees of freedom. It appears whenever we sum squared deviations: the sample variance from a normal population satisfies (n − 1)s²/σ² ~ χ²(n − 1), and the goodness-of-fit statistic of chapter 11 is approximately chi-square as well. It is right-skewed, with mean k and variance 2k, and becomes more symmetric as k grows.

    The ratio of two independent chi-square variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom, has an F distribution. Since sample variances are scaled chi-squares, F is the distribution of a ratio of two variances, which is exactly what the analysis of variance in chapter 12 compares.
