Analysis of variance

The t-test compares two means. With three or more groups, running every pairwise test inflates the chance of a false alarm. ANOVA asks the question once, by comparing how much the group means differ with how much the data scatter within groups.

In this chapter

    Between-group and within-group variation

    Take k groups of n observations each. The total variation of the data around the grand mean splits exactly into two pieces: the variation of the group means around the grand mean (between, SSB), and the variation of the observations around their own group means (within, SSW). If all groups share the same true mean, the between-group piece is just more sampling noise and should be about the same size, per degree of freedom, as the within-group piece.

    F=SSB/(k−1)SSW/(N−k)

    The ratio F is near 1 when the null hypothesis of equal means is true and grows when the means really differ. Under H₀ it follows the F distribution of chapter 7 with k − 1 and N − k degrees of freedom, so a p-value follows in the usual way.

    Try this

    Set all three true means to 0 and repeat 1,000 times: F follows the green curve and about 5% of experiments reject. Move one mean to 1 with σ = 1 and n = 10; the rejection rate jumps. Then raise σ to 2 and watch the same difference disappear into the noise.

    Check yourself: why not just run three t-tests?

    Each test has a 5% false-alarm rate. Three of them together raise the chance of at least one false alarm to about 14%, and with ten groups (45 pairs) it exceeds 90%. ANOVA controls the overall rate with a single test; if it rejects, follow-up comparisons can be made with appropriate corrections.
