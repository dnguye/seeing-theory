Probability & Statistics 2 · Chapter 13

Regression

When one variable helps predict another, a line through the scatter summarises the relationship. Least squares picks the line, correlation grades how well it fits, and the machinery of chapters 7 to 10 says how much to trust its slope.

In this chapter

    Ordinary least squares

    Given pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ), we want a line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x that comes closest to the points. “Closest” needs a definition: least squares uses the vertical gaps, the residuals eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ, and chooses the line that minimises the sum of their squares. Calculus gives closed-form answers:

    b1=∑(xi−x¯)(yi−y¯)∑(xi−x¯)2,b0=y¯−b1x¯

    The line always passes through the point of means (x̄, ȳ), and the residuals always sum to zero. Squaring makes distant points count for a lot: drag one point far from the others and watch it pull the line toward itself. The “squared residuals” view draws each residual as an actual square, so you can see the total area the line is minimising.

    Try this

    Drag a single point to the top-left corner. The slope flips and R² collapses: one outlier can dominate a least-squares fit. Double-click a point to delete it. Then drag the points into a curve and notice that a straight line can fit badly while still being “the best line”.

    Correlation and R²

    The sample correlation r is the covariance of chapter 5, computed from data and scaled to lie in [−1, 1]. It is tied to the fitted slope by b₁ = r · sᵧ/sₓ: the slope is the correlation in the units of the data. Its square, R², has a direct interpretation as the fraction of the variation in y that the line accounts for. The total sum of squares of y around its mean splits, exactly as in ANOVA, into a part explained by the regression and a residual part, and R² is the explained fraction.

    R² = 0.9 does not mean the line is correct, only that residuals are small relative to the spread of y. And correlation is not causation: ice-cream sales and drownings are correlated because both follow the summer.

    Inference for the slope

    The fitted slope b₁ is a statistic, so it has a sampling distribution. If the true relationship is y = β₀ + β₁x + noise with normal noise of standard deviation σ, then b₁ is normal with mean β₁ (it is unbiased) and standard deviation σ/√Σ(xᵢ − x̄)². Spread-out x values pin the slope down; noisy y values loosen it. Replacing σ by the residual standard deviation gives a standard error, and a t interval with n − 2 degrees of freedom follows exactly as for a mean.

    The lab draws sample after sample from a known line, fits each one, and stacks the slopes. Every fitted line is a little wrong, the histogram of slopes is a bell around the truth, and about 95% of the intervals contain 0.5.
